# Why Mixing It Up Beats Repetition: The Science of Interleaved Practice

*Reading time: about 8 minutes*

Here is a pattern almost everyone falls into. You want to get better at something — a set of math problems, a language's verb tenses, the chords on a guitar, the way three similar AI tools each behave — so you drill one piece until it feels solid, then move to the next, then the next. It feels efficient. Each block clicks into place before you move on.

There is decades of research suggesting that this comfortable, block-by-block approach is quietly one of the least effective ways to build lasting skill. A messier-feeling alternative — mixing different but related problems together in a single session — tends to produce learning that sticks longer and transfers better to new situations. It is called **interleaved practice**, and once you understand why it works, you will start to see missed opportunities for it everywhere.

## Blocked practice vs. interleaved practice

The two approaches are easy to define.

**Blocked practice** means you group all of one kind together: `AAAA BBBB CCCC`. Ten problems of type A, then ten of type B, then ten of type C. You finish one topic before starting the next.

**Interleaved practice** means you shuffle the types together: `A B C A C B B A C`. You are constantly switching between related skills within the same session.

Blocked practice almost always *feels* better while you are doing it. Your performance during a block looks smooth and improving, because once you have identified which kind of problem you are facing, you can apply the same procedure again and again on autopilot. Interleaving feels worse: you make more mistakes, you hesitate, progress looks bumpy. That discomfort is exactly the point — and it is also why so few people stick with it.

## Why the harder-feeling method works better

The counterintuitive finding is that the very thing that makes interleaving feel worse in the moment is what makes it work in the long run. Cognitive scientists call these "desirable difficulties" — challenges that slow you down during practice but strengthen the learning underneath.

Interleaving appears to help for a few related reasons:

**It forces you to choose the right approach, not just execute it.** In a blocked set, the choice is made for you — every problem is type A, so you never have to *decide* what kind of problem it is. In real life, and on any real test, nobody labels the problem for you. Interleaving builds the skill you actually need: recognizing what kind of situation you are in and retrieving the matching strategy. That act of discrimination is the part blocked practice skips entirely.

**It spaces your practice automatically.** Because you keep returning to type A after visiting B and C, each return is a small act of retrieval from a slightly colder memory. That built-in spacing is closely related to why spaced repetition works — a topic worth its own read if you want the timing side of the story ([how to use AI for spaced repetition](/how-to-use-ai-for-spaced-repetition/)).

**It sharpens the contrasts between similar things.** When confusable categories sit side by side, your brain has to notice what actually distinguishes them. That is why interleaving shines precisely where blocked practice fails: telling apart things that look alike.

## What the research actually shows — and what it doesn't

It is worth being precise here, because interleaving gets oversold as a magic upgrade for all learning. The honest version is more useful.

In a well-known set of studies on math practice, students who interleaved different problem types substantially outperformed those who blocked their practice — but the advantage showed up on a *later test*, not during practice itself. During practice, the blocked group often looked like it was doing better. The benefit was hidden until it counted.

In another classic study, people learning to recognize different painters' styles learned to identify new paintings more accurately when examples of different artists were interleaved rather than shown one artist at a time — even though most participants *believed* the blocked approach had helped them more. The gap between what felt effective and what was effective is one of the most consistent themes in this research.

Now the caveats, because they matter:

- Interleaving helps most when the things you are mixing are **related and easily confused** — different problem types in the same subject, similar grammar rules, lookalike categories. Mixing completely unrelated tasks (French verbs and free-throws) does not carry the same benefit and can just be distracting.
- It is largely about **practice and retrieval**, not first exposure. You still need a clear initial explanation of each idea before shuffling them makes sense.
- The effect sizes vary by domain and study. Interleaving is a reliable, well-replicated tool — not a guarantee of a fixed percentage gain. Treat claims of a precise "X% improvement" with healthy skepticism.

Used within those limits, interleaving is one of the best-supported, lowest-cost changes you can make to how you practice.

## How to actually use it

You do not need to overhaul anything. You need to stop sorting your practice into tidy blocks.

**Mix problem types within a session.** If you are working through exercises, don't do all of chapter 3 then all of chapter 4. Pull a handful from each and shuffle them. The moment of "wait, which method does this one need?" is the rep that matters.

**Rotate related skills.** Learning a language? Alternate between tenses in the same drill instead of conjugating one tense twenty times. Learning an instrument? Rotate the pieces or techniques you are working on rather than looping one until it is perfect and then abandoning it.

**Embrace the bumpiness.** Your in-session accuracy will drop, and it will feel like you are doing worse. You are not. You are trading a smooth-feeling practice session for stronger memory later. If it feels a little frustrating, you are probably doing it right.

**Combine it with feedback.** Interleaving tells you *how to arrange* practice; it doesn't replace knowing *whether you got it right*. Pair it with tight feedback loops — the core of [deliberate practice](/the-science-of-deliberate-practice/) — so your mixed reps are also corrected reps.

## Where AI fits in

This is where a good AI tool earns its place — not by doing the thinking for you, but by removing the friction that stops people from interleaving in the first place.

The main reason people default to blocked practice is that building a genuinely mixed, well-shuffled problem set by hand is tedious. AI makes it nearly free. You can ask a chatbot to *"generate 15 practice problems drawn from these four topics, shuffled in random order, without telling me which topic each one is"* — and get exactly the kind of set that forces you to identify before you solve. You can ask it to produce lookalike examples of two concepts you keep confusing, side by side, so you have to spot the difference.

The key is to keep the *retrieval* on your side of the desk. Let the AI build the mixed practice; you do the recall, the choosing, and the checking. Hand it the generation, not the thinking. ## The takeaway

Repetition feels like progress, and that feeling is exactly what misleads us. The practice that feels smoothest is often the one that fades fastest. Mixing related skills together — interleaving — feels harder, exposes your gaps, and forces you to choose the right tool for each moment. That is precisely why it builds knowledge that lasts and transfers.

The next time you sit down to practice anything, resist the urge to line it all up in neat blocks. Shuffle the deck. Your future self, facing an unlabeled problem, will thank you.

**Key takeaways**

- **Blocked practice** (`AAAA BBBB`) feels productive but builds fragile, autopilot skill; **interleaved practice** (`A B C A`) feels harder but builds durable, transferable skill.
- Interleaving works by forcing you to *choose* the right strategy, spacing your retrieval automatically, and sharpening the contrasts between similar ideas.
- It helps most for **related, easily-confused material**, shows its benefit on *later tests* (not during practice), and is not a universal upgrade for every kind of learning.
- Expect practice to feel bumpier — that discomfort is a "desirable difficulty," not a sign you are doing it wrong.
- AI is ideal for the tedious part: generating shuffled, unlabeled, mixed practice sets. Keep the retrieval and judgment on your side.

**Try this today:** Take whatever you are currently practicing and build one mixed set — pull a few items from three different sub-topics and shuffle them so you can't predict what's next. Do that set instead of your usual block, and notice how different it feels to have to identify each item before you tackle it.
