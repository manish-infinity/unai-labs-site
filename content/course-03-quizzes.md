The on-brand AI angle appears where the course raises it (AI/tech as a sleep and stress variable, and AI as a planning aid whose reps still have to be yours).

No question references unpublished content; "go deeper" links in the lessons point only to already-published brain-health posts (68 sleep, 83 stress, 90 exercise, 96 nutrition).

## Module 1 Knowledge Check — What Brain Health Actually Is

### Q1. (Multiple choice) Which statement best describes what your brain actually is?

- A. A fixed piece of hardware whose sharpness is set by genetics and can't really be changed.
- B. A physical, energy-hungry organ fed by your bloodstream, whose function lives in trillions of changeable connections. **[CORRECT]**
- C. A muscle that gets stronger only through brain-training games.
- D. Mostly unused — we only run about 10% of it.

**Answer:** B. The brain is a physical organ that runs on blood-borne oxygen and glucose, which is why what's good for your heart is generally good for your brain. Its function lives in the changeable connections between neurons, so sharpness is not a fixed trait — it rises and falls with daily inputs you control. (A overstates genetics; C is the brain-training myth; D is the "10% of your brain" myth, addressed directly in Lesson 1.3.)

### Q2. (Multiple choice) Course 3 is built around "five levers" that do most of the work in brain health. What are they?

- A. Supplements, brain-training apps, nootropics, cold plunges, and fasting.
- B. Genetics, age, luck, income, and education.
- C. Sleep, movement, nutrition, stress, and connection. **[CORRECT]**
- D. Focus, memory, mood, creativity, and willpower.

**Answer:** C. The five everyday levers are sleep, movement, nutrition, stress, and connection. They function as an interconnected system — each one strengthens or undermines the others — and getting these fundamentals roughly right is the 80/20 of brain health. (A is the marketing-driven "optimization" list the course explicitly de-prioritizes; D lists *outcomes* the levers produce, not the levers themselves.)

### Q3. (True/False) The five levers act independently, so you can safely ignore four of them as long as you optimize one.

- A. True
- B. False **[CORRECT]**

**Answer:** False. The levers are an interconnected system: poor sleep raises stress and undermines food choices; movement improves sleep; connection buffers stress. Because of this, your *weakest* lever is your highest-leverage target — improving it lifts the whole system — so the goal is to get all five roughly right, not to max out one.

### Q4. (Multiple choice) A product promises to "unlock the 90% of your brain you're not using." What's the science-honest response?

- A. Worth trying — most people really do use only about 10% of their brain.
- B. It's a myth; you use all of your brain, and any claim promising hidden brainpower deserves suspicion. **[CORRECT]**
- C. True only for people who don't exercise.
- D. True, but only the unused part controls memory.

**Answer:** B. The "10% of your brain" idea is a myth — you use all of it. Lesson 1.3 uses claims like this as a filter: anything promising secret, effortless, hidden brainpower is a marketing signal, not a science signal. The highest-leverage habits are unglamorous, well-supported, and mostly free.

### Q5. (Multiple choice) Which best captures the course's core stance on how brain health actually improves?

- A. It's a one-time fix you buy once and forget.
- B. It's mostly genetic, so habits barely matter.
- C. It's a set of repeatable daily habits — most of them free — not a supplement or a single purchase. **[CORRECT]**
- D. It requires expensive equipment and a gym membership.

**Answer:** C. Brain health is a set of habits, not a product. Most of the highest-leverage habits (consistent sleep, daily movement, a decent dietary pattern, stress recovery, real connection) cost nothing. Optimizations can come later — after the fundamentals, not instead of them.

## Module 2 Knowledge Check — Sleep: The Brain's Maintenance System

### Q1. (Multiple choice) What is the best description of what sleep does for the brain?

- A. Nothing much — it's just downtime when the brain switches off.
- B. Active maintenance: deep sleep runs a glymphatic "wash cycle" that clears metabolic waste, and the brain consolidates the day's learning. **[CORRECT]**
- C. It only rests the muscles, not the brain.
- D. It erases the day's memories to make room for tomorrow.

**Answer:** B. Sleep is active maintenance, not downtime. During deep sleep the glymphatic system clears metabolic waste (including amyloid-beta), and overnight the brain consolidates memories, moving them from fragile short-term into durable long-term storage. (D is the opposite of consolidation — sleep *strengthens* the day's important memories rather than erasing them.)

### Q2. (True/False) If you lose sleep during the week, you can fully repay the debt by sleeping in on the weekend.

- A. True
- B. False **[CORRECT]**

**Answer:** False. You can recover *some* lost sleep, but not all of it — chronic short sleep can't be fully repaid later. A weekend catch-up helps a little but doesn't undo the accumulated cost, which is why protecting sleep nightly matters more than trying to bank or repay it.

### Q3. (Multiple choice) According to Module 2, what is the single highest-leverage sleep habit?

- A. A consistent wake time every day — weekends included. **[CORRECT]**
- B. Taking a melatonin supplement each night.
- C. Getting exactly eight hours no matter what.
- D. Sleeping in whenever you feel tired.

**Answer:** A. A consistent wake time anchors your whole circadian clock, which is why it's the highest-leverage habit — more than chasing an exact number of hours or reaching for a supplement. Light is the other master signal: bright light early, dim evenings, and less screen light in the last hour.

### Q4. (Multiple choice) Which set are the biggest, most-adjustable saboteurs of restoring sleep?

- A. Reading, stretching, a cool room, and a dark room.
- B. Late caffeine, alcohol, evening screens, and an irregular schedule. **[CORRECT]**
- C. Morning sunlight, daytime movement, and social contact.
- D. A consistent wake time and a short wind-down routine.

**Answer:** B. The four biggest saboteurs are late caffeine, alcohol, evening screens, and an irregular schedule — all adjustable, none requiring a purchase. Note that alcohol can knock you out but *fragments* sleep quality. (A and D describe habits that *help* sleep, not sabotage it.)

### Q5. (True/False) When sleep won't come, the calmest evidence-based move is to stay in bed and try harder to force it.

- A. True
- B. False **[CORRECT]**

**Answer:** False. Forcing sleep tends to raise arousal and make it worse. The troubleshooting approach in Lesson 2.3 is to reduce panic and pressure — protect the wind-down, get up and do something calm and dim if you're wide awake rather than lying there frustrated, and rely on the consistent wake time to reset the system — instead of white-knuckling it in bed.

## Module 3 Knowledge Check — Fuel and Movement: The Body-Brain Connection

### Q1. (Multiple choice) Why does physical movement matter so directly for the brain?

- A. It doesn't — exercise only affects the body, not the brain.
- B. The brain runs on blood-borne oxygen and glucose with no reserve, and movement improves the circulation that feeds it. **[CORRECT]**
- C. It burns off the brain cells you don't need.
- D. It only helps if you train for hours in a gym.

**Answer:** B. The brain has essentially no fuel reserve and depends on steady blood-borne oxygen and glucose, so movement — by improving circulation and vascular health — directly supports the tissue doing your thinking. It also raises BDNF, discussed next. (D is false: moving more beats moving none; a daily walk plus a little strength work, or "exercise snacks" on busy weeks, still counts.)

### Q2. (Multiple choice) Exercise raises BDNF. What is BDNF best described as, and what does it do?

- A. A stress hormone that shrinks the brain.
- B. A "fertilizer" for brain cells that supports new connections, especially in the memory-central hippocampus. **[CORRECT]**
- C. A supplement you should buy to improve memory.
- D. A type of brain-training game.

**Answer:** B. BDNF acts like fertilizer for brain cells: it supports the growth and maintenance of connections, particularly in the hippocampus, which is central to memory. A single bout of movement also lifts mood, buffers stress, sharpens focus that same day, and improves that night's sleep — the levers reinforcing each other.

### Q3. (Multiple choice) What does the evidence actually favour for eating well for your brain?

- A. One specific "brain superfood" eaten daily.
- B. An expensive supplement stack.
- C. An overall dietary pattern — the Mediterranean pattern most of all — rather than any single food or supplement. **[CORRECT]**
- D. Cutting out all carbohydrates.

**Answer:** C. Overall dietary *patterns* beat any single "brain food" or supplement, with the Mediterranean-style pattern the best-supported. Steadying blood sugar (pairing carbs with protein, fat, and fibre) and supporting the gut with fibre and a variety of plants matter more than chasing one miracle ingredient. Omega-3s are best obtained from food. (D is not what the lesson recommends.)

### Q4. (True/False) Even mild dehydration can measurably affect concentration and mental performance.

- A. True **[CORRECT]**
- B. False

**Answer:** True. The brain is sensitive to hydration, and even mild dehydration can dent concentration and mood. It's one of the cheapest, most overlooked levers — often mistaken for tiredness or an afternoon slump.

### Q5. (Multiple choice) What's the realistic way to "put the body-brain habits together" from Lesson 3.3?

- A. Overhaul your diet and training all at once and rely on willpower to sustain it.
- B. Layer small, sustainable habits — move a bit daily, steady your meals, stay hydrated — chosen for consistency over intensity. **[CORRECT]**
- C. Only bother on days you have time for a full workout and a perfect meal.
- D. Wait until you can afford a gym and a nutritionist.

**Answer:** B. The lesson's whole point is that consistency beats intensity and that small, repeatable habits compound. Moving more beats moving none; steadier meals beat a "perfect" diet you can't maintain; hydration is nearly free. An all-or-nothing overhaul (A, C) is exactly the pattern that doesn't last.

## Module 4 Knowledge Check — Stress, Mood, Connection & Cognitive Longevity

### Q1. (Multiple choice) Is stress bad for the brain?

- A. Always — any stress at all damages the brain.
- B. Never — stress has no effect on the brain.
- C. It depends: short, recoverable (acute) stress sharpens focus, while chronic, never-switching-off stress is the harmful kind. **[CORRECT]**
- D. Only physical stress matters; mental stress is harmless.

**Answer:** C. Not all stress is harmful. Short, acute, recoverable stress sharpens focus — the system working as designed. The problem is *chronic* stress: never fully switching off keeps cortisol elevated, which erodes the memory-central hippocampus and makes learning harder. It also drags down the other levers (sleep, food, movement, connection), which makes it especially high-leverage to address.

### Q2. (Multiple choice) Module 4 recommends buffers for stress that are "free and familiar." Which is a good example for an acute stress spike?

- A. Slow breathing with a longer exhale. **[CORRECT]**
- B. An expensive supplement.
- C. A late-night coffee to push through.
- D. Ignoring it until it goes away.

**Answer:** A. Slow breathing with a longer exhale directly calms the acute stress response — it's free and always available. For chronic load, the buffers are boundaries that protect recovery and the other levers (sleep, movement, connection). The best stress tools don't need to be bought.

### Q3. (True/False) Social connection is a genuine predictor of how well the brain ages — on a par with the physical levers.

- A. True **[CORRECT]**
- B. False

**Answer:** True. The brain is a social organ, and how connected you are is a real predictor of cognitive aging, comparable to the physical levers. Chronic loneliness acts as an ongoing stressor and is linked to faster cognitive decline and higher dementia risk. Meaningful contact and a sense of purpose are protective — conversation is a demanding whole-brain workout.

### Q4. (Multiple choice) What is "cognitive reserve," and how is it built?

- A. A fixed amount of brainpower you're born with that only declines.
- B. Built-up neural resilience — richer connections that keep the brain functioning well as it ages — which is built, not fixed at birth. **[CORRECT]**
- C. A savings account you can only spend, never grow.
- D. Something only genetics determines.

**Answer:** B. Cognitive reserve is your brain's accumulated resilience — more and richer neural connections that help it keep functioning well with age. Crucially, it's *built*, not fixed at birth: every lever feeds it (movement grows connections, sleep consolidates them, low stress and strong ties protect them, and learning spends them well).

### Q5. (Multiple choice) What's the best-supported way to keep building cognitive reserve over a lifetime?

- A. Grinding daily brain-training apps.
- B. Genuine novelty and lifelong learning — real curiosity, not app-based drills. **[CORRECT]**
- C. Buying more supplements as you age.
- D. Avoiding anything mentally challenging to "preserve" the brain.

**Answer:** B. Genuine novelty and lifelong learning are the best-supported way to keep building reserve — brain-training apps are *not*. Curiosity is itself a brain-health practice. And the plan that works is the one you keep: one small, anchored, forgiving habit per lever, chosen for consistency over intensity and left to compound over years. (D is backwards — challenge builds reserve; avoidance doesn't preserve it.)

## Module 4 Capstone — Self-Evaluation Rubric (Your One-Page Brain Health Plan)

The Module 4 capstone is a **one-page personal Brain Health Plan**: one small, anchored, forgiving habit for each of the five levers, plus how you'll keep it going. This rubric is for self-evaluation (or light instructor review) — it is not auto-graded. Score each item 0–2 (0 = not yet, 1 = partly, 2 = yes); a solid plan scores 10+/12.

1. **Covers all five levers.** The plan names one concrete habit for each of sleep, movement, nutrition, stress, and connection — none skipped. (0–2)
2. **Starts from your weakest lever.** The plan identifies which lever is currently weakest and gives it real attention, since that's the highest-leverage target. (0–2)
3. **Small and anchored, not heroic.** Each habit is small enough to do on a bad day and is anchored to an existing routine (e.g. "after I pour my morning coffee, I…"), chosen for consistency over intensity. (0–2)
4. **Forgiving by design.** The plan includes a "get back on track" rule for missed days (no all-or-nothing collapse) rather than assuming perfection. (0–2)
5. **Free / low-cost and realistic for an ordinary week.** No dependence on supplements, gadgets, or a schedule you can't actually keep; the plan fits a normal week. (0–2)
6. **Has a keep-going mechanism.** The plan says how you'll track it and when you'll review/adjust — acknowledging that reserve is built by habits left to compound over years, not a one-time push. (0–2)

**Passing reflection prompt:** In two sentences, which single lever, if you improved it for one month, would most lift the other four — and what is the smallest version of that habit you're confident you'll actually keep?
