## Module 1 Knowledge Check — How Your Brain Actually Changes
*Covers Lessons 1.1–1.3. Passing grade 80% (4/5).*

### Q1. (Multiple choice) What is neuroplasticity, accurately stated?
- A. A special talent that only some gifted people are born with
- B. The brain's lifelong ability to reorganise itself by strengthening, forming, and pruning connections in response to what you do **[CORRECT]**
- C. Something that switches off in your mid-twenties
- D. The ability to add new neurons at will to raise your IQ

*Explanation:* Plasticity is the **default operating mode of every human brain, across the lifespan** — not a rare gift (A) and not something that ends in early adulthood (C). "Rewiring" is literal: connections you use get stronger, connections you don't get pruned.

### Q2. (Multiple choice) The course names four conditions that drive rewiring. They are:
- A. Talent, luck, age, and genetics
- B. Attention, effort, rest, and repetition **[CORRECT]**
- C. Caffeine, deadlines, stress, and cramming
- D. Reading, highlighting, summarising, and re-reading

*Explanation:* Rewiring needs **attention** (the brain changes what you focus on), genuine **effort** (the stimulus), **rest** (when change consolidates — Module 4), and **repetition** (which strengthens the pathway). Notice that cramming and highlighting (C, D) are close to the *opposite* of these.

### Q3. (True/False) "You only use 10% of your brain," so most of your neural capacity is sitting idle.
- A. True
- B. False **[CORRECT]**

*Explanation:* Lesson 1.3 exists to separate real science from hype, and the "10% of your brain" claim is a **myth** — imaging shows essentially all of the brain is active over a day. The honest message is more empowering than the myth: your brain is already fully engaged, and it's the *wiring* that changes with practice.

### Q4. (Multiple choice) Why does a new skill feel effortful at first and automatic later?
- A. Your brain runs out of effort and stops caring
- B. You are literally strengthening the synaptic connections you use, so signals pass more easily over time **[CORRECT]**
- C. The skill was always easy; you only imagined the difficulty
- D. "Automatic" means you've stopped thinking altogether

*Explanation:* The physical picture from Module 1: "**neurons that fire together, wire together.**" The felt shift from effortful to automatic *is* neuroplasticity happening — which is why the course treats effort as a feature, not a nuisance.

### Q5. (Multiple choice) What is the practical upshot of "your learning ability is not fixed"?
- A. You can skip practice because talent decides everything
- B. Because ability is a product of habits, better strategies practised consistently become just as automatic as bad ones are now **[CORRECT]**
- C. Once you're an adult, changing study habits is pointless
- D. Only children can meaningfully learn new skills

*Explanation:* Lesson 1.1's takeaway cuts both ways: ineffective habits (re-reading, highlighting) are hard to drop because they're deeply wired — but that **same** plasticity means better habits, repeated, will wire in too. You're not stuck; you're wired, and wiring can be redone.

## Module 2 Knowledge Check — Learning Faster & Remembering Longer
*Covers Lessons 2.1–2.4. Passing grade 80% (4/5).*

### Q1. (Multiple choice) Why does the course call forgetting "a feature," not a flaw?
- A. Because forgetting means your brain is damaged
- B. Because the brain discards information it gets no signal it will need again — so forgetting is lawful, and therefore beatable **[CORRECT]**
- C. Because nothing can be done about the forgetting curve
- D. Because long-term memory has a small, fixed capacity that fills up

*Explanation:* The forgetting curve is steep — much of what you learn is gone within a day without reinforcement — but it's the **normal** behaviour of a healthy system that keeps what it gets signals to keep. Because it's lawful, the right techniques can defeat it. (Long-term memory capacity is effectively unlimited, so D is wrong.)

### Q2. (Multiple choice) Why is spaced repetition described as "the highest-leverage learning tool there is"?
- A. It lets you cram everything the night before more efficiently
- B. Revisiting material across spaced sessions produces dramatically more durable learning than the same total time massed together **[CORRECT]**
- C. It works by re-reading the same page many times in a row
- D. It removes the need to ever recall anything

*Explanation:* Spacing the same study time across days beats cramming it into one block for long-term retention — you're repeatedly reminding the brain "this matters" right as it's about to forget. Cramming *rents* knowledge for a day; spacing *builds* it to last.

### Q3. (Multiple choice) What is "active recall," and why does it beat re-reading?
- A. Reading the material again, more slowly and carefully
- B. Pulling information out of memory (testing yourself), which strengthens the pathway far more than putting information back in **[CORRECT]**
- C. Highlighting the most important sentences
- D. Listening to the lecture a second time

*Explanation:* **Retrieval isn't just a test of memory — it's one of the strongest ways to build it.** Re-reading creates a fluency the brain mistakes for understanding; effortful recall does the actual work. This is where **desirable difficulty** comes in.

### Q4. (True/False) If a study session feels smooth and easy, that's a reliable sign it's working well.
- A. True
- B. False **[CORRECT]**

*Explanation:* The **desirable-difficulty** principle: durable strategies feel *harder* in the moment, because effortful, slightly frustrating retrieval is the signal that tells your brain to keep the material. Smoothness usually means you're recognising, not recalling — a warning sign, not a win.

### Q5. (Multiple choice) What does "building a personal learning system that sticks" combine?
- A. One perfect app that does the learning for you
- B. Retrieval + spacing + elaboration + rest, arranged into a routine you'll actually repeat **[CORRECT]**
- C. Longer and longer single study sessions
- D. Motivation alone — waiting until you feel inspired

*Explanation:* Lesson 2.4 assembles the module: recall effortfully, space it over time, connect new ideas to what you already know (**elaboration**), and let rest consolidate it (Module 4). A system beats willpower because **consistency, not intensity,** is what wires learning in.

## Module 3 Knowledge Check — Focus & Attention as Trainable Skills
*Covers Lessons 3.1–3.3. Passing grade 80% (4/5).*

### Q1. (Multiple choice) "Attention is a muscle" — what does that metaphor correctly capture?
- A. Attention is fixed and can't be improved
- B. Attention is a trainable capacity built through use and recovery — and its real "reps" are the acts of noticing distraction and returning **[CORRECT]**
- C. Attention, like a muscle, can be grown to unlimited size
- D. You should push attention to failure every session

*Explanation:* The useful part of the metaphor is that focus is a **trainable capacity, not a fixed trait**, and it needs both effort and recovery. Kept science-honest, the actual rep is catching that your mind wandered and bringing it back.

### Q2. (Multiple choice) In a "distraction economy," what's the most effective way to rebuild your focus span?
- A. Rely on willpower to resist your phone in the moment
- B. Change the environment — remove the option and add friction to distraction — so you're not spending discipline fighting every urge **[CORRECT]**
- C. Multitask more, so you get used to switching
- D. Wait until you feel motivated to focus

*Explanation:* Apps are engineered to win the in-the-moment willpower fight, so the leverage is **environmental**: put the phone out of reach, close the tab, remove the choice. You design distraction out rather than out-muscling it each time.

### Q3. (True/False) True multitasking on two demanding tasks is efficient once you're used to it.
- A. True
- B. False **[CORRECT]**

*Explanation:* For demanding work, "multitasking" is really **fast task-switching**, and each switch carries a real reconfiguration cost plus **attention residue** (part of your mind stuck on the last task). Switching is genuinely costly — which is why deliberate single-tasking is the method. (The course also notes that popular figures like an "IQ drop" or a flat "23 minutes to refocus" are overstated; the robust point is that switch costs are real.)

### Q4. (Multiple choice) What actually makes a "deep work session" work?
- A. Working as many hours as possible in one heroic stretch
- B. A protected, single-task block of realistic length, with distractions made unavailable — repeated consistently **[CORRECT]**
- C. Keeping several tabs and chats open so you don't miss anything
- D. Powering through without ever taking a break

*Explanation:* Deep work is a **container**: one task, a length you can actually finish, everything else genuinely out of reach. **Consistency beats intensity** — a short block done daily trains attention far better than a rare marathon that mostly teaches you focus is miserable.

### Q5. (Multiple choice) During focused work, your mind wanders. What's the trained response?
- A. Criticise yourself for losing focus so you learn not to
- B. Notice the drift and gently return your attention — the noticing-and-returning *is* the rep **[CORRECT]**
- C. Give up the session; you've broken your focus
- D. Switch to a different task until focus comes back on its own

*Explanation:* Wandering isn't failure — it's the **setup for the rep**. You catch it and return, without self-punishment (which just spends attention on frustration). Over time the returns get faster and the drifts less frequent.

## Module 4 Knowledge Check — Resilience & Cognitive Longevity
*Covers Lessons 4.1–4.3. Passing grade 80% (4/5).*

### Q1. (Multiple choice) "Rest is the rewiring." What role does sleep play in learning?
- A. Sleep is downtime that has nothing to do with memory
- B. Consolidation happens largely during rest and sleep — it's when freshly encoded, fragile memories are stabilised and integrated **[CORRECT]**
- C. You can fully replace sleep with more study time
- D. Only the hours before midnight count for memory

*Explanation:* Encoding gets information *in*, but **consolidation** — much of it during sleep — is what makes it stick. Skip the rest and you undercut everything you studied; consolidation can't be rushed or skipped.

### Q2. (True/False) A lost night of sleep can be fully "paid back" later with no cost to learning or brain health.
- A. True
- B. False **[CORRECT]**

*Explanation:* Some recovery is possible, but lost sleep isn't a debt you can fully repay, and chronic short sleep degrades the very **consolidation and attention** that learning depends on. Protecting sleep is a high-leverage move, not an optional extra.

### Q3. (Multiple choice) How does chronic stress affect the brain's ability to change?
- A. It has no measurable effect on learning
- B. Chronically elevated cortisol impairs memory and the capacity to rewire — so managing stress protects plasticity itself **[CORRECT]**
- C. More stress always means better focus and faster learning
- D. Stress affects mood only, not memory

*Explanation:* The course distinguishes **acute** stress (can sharpen short-term focus) from **chronic** stress (corrosive): sustained high cortisol harms the hippocampus and the conditions for rewiring. Protecting your brain's ability to change means keeping chronic stress in check.

### Q4. (Multiple choice) What is "cognitive reserve," and what best builds it?
- A. A fixed amount of intelligence you're born with
- B. A resilience buffer built over a lifetime — best strengthened by novelty and continued learning, not by any single brain-training app **[CORRECT]**
- C. Something only crossword puzzles can build
- D. A supplement you can take to protect the brain

*Explanation:* Cognitive reserve is the brain's **resilience against age and decline**, built by lifelong learning, novelty, meaningful engagement, and physical activity — not by one "brain-training" app, whose gains tend not to transfer beyond the game itself.

### Q5. (Multiple choice) What is the overarching message of Module 4 for a resilient brain?
- A. Push harder and sleep less to learn more
- B. The brain's ability to change rests on a physiological floor — sleep, stress, movement — so protecting that floor is the long game **[CORRECT]**
- C. Cognitive decline is inevitable and nothing helps
- D. Genetics fully determine your brain's future

*Explanation:* The module reframes resilience as a **floor you protect**: no learning technique overcomes a chronically exhausted or stressed brain. Sleep, stress management, movement, and lifelong novelty are what keep plasticity available for the long run. (This is also where the course hands off cleanly to Brain Health 101.)

## Module 4 Capstone — Self-Evaluation Rubric (not a Tutor quiz)
*Add this as the final lesson/assignment description. The capstone asks the learner to combine the course's methods into a **one-page personal learning system**. There's no single right answer, so learners self-score against the rubric below. A strong system hits all six.*

Score each item Yes / Partly / Not yet. Aim for six "Yes."

1. **A correct model (Module 1):** Does your plan treat learning ability as **changeable** — built from habits, driven by attention, effort, rest, and repetition — rather than a fixed trait?
2. **Retrieval over re-reading (Module 2):** Is **active recall** (testing yourself) your default study move, replacing passive re-reading and highlighting?
3. **Spacing built in (Module 2):** Have you scheduled review **across days/weeks** rather than massing study into one session?
4. **Embracing desirable difficulty (Modules 2–3):** Does your plan expect study to feel **effortful** — and treat smooth, easy sessions as a warning sign, not a success?
5. **Protected focus blocks (Module 3):** Have you designed a distraction-resistant, **single-task** deep-work block of realistic length, with a "notice and return" habit for when your mind wanders?
6. **Protecting the floor (Module 4):** Does your system protect **sleep**, manage **chronic stress**, and include **movement/novelty** — so your brain can actually consolidate and keep changing?

*If you scored "Not yet" on any item, revisit that module's lesson — the fix is there. Your one-page system is what you leave the course with; keep it where you'll see it.*
