Retrieval practice (being tested on what you just read) is one of the most robustly supported findings in the learning science this brand teaches — it strengthens memory far more than re-reading — so a short knowledge check at the end of each module is not busywork; it *is* part of the method. Each quiz below is deliberately built to make the learner retrieve the module's load-bearing ideas, and every question's feedback reinforces the science-honest framing (what the evidence supports vs. the popular over-claims).

## Module 1 Knowledge Check — How Attention Actually Works
*Covers Lessons 1.1-1.3. Passing grade 80% (4/5).*

### Q1. (Multiple choice) What is the best description of what "attention" actually is?
- A. A fixed personality trait — some people are born focused and some aren't
- B. A mood that comes and goes depending on the day
- C. The brain's mechanism for selecting a small slice of everything coming in to process, while suppressing the rest **[CORRECT]**
- D. The total amount of information your senses can take in at once

*Explanation:* Attention is selection under scarcity. Your senses take in far more than the brain can process, so attention acts as a narrow spotlight — illuminating a little while pushing the rest into the background. It is neither a fixed trait (A) nor a random mood (B); framing it that way is exactly the mistake the course is built to correct.

### Q2. (Multiple choice) Attention is often described as three cooperating systems. When someone says "I just can't focus," which system are they usually describing?
- A. Alerting — the general state of being awake and ready
- B. Orienting — aiming the spotlight at something
- C. Executive control — holding the spotlight on what you chose and returning it when it drifts **[CORRECT]**
- D. Sensory processing — how sharply the eyes and ears detect input

*Explanation:* Alerting (readiness) and orienting (aiming) matter, but "I can't focus" almost always means the *executive control* system feels weak — the effortful job of holding attention against distraction and pulling it back. That's also the system that responds most to training, which is why the whole course targets it.

### Q3. (True/False) The claim that "the human attention span has dropped to 8 seconds — shorter than a goldfish" is well-established neuroscience.
- A. True
- B. False **[CORRECT]**

*Explanation:* This popular statistic is not real science — it traces back to an unsourced figure with no credible research behind it (and "attention span" isn't a single measurable number that could drop like that). The honest picture is that attention is an *untrained* skill under *environmental siege*, not a species-wide biological collapse. The brand's rule: build your understanding on what the evidence supports, not on viral figures.

### Q4. (Multiple choice) Why does focusing on something that isn't intrinsically gripping — a report, a hard problem — feel like genuine effort?
- A. Because willpower is a fixed daily quantity that runs out on a strict schedule
- B. Because selecting one thing means actively suppressing everything else, which draws on the same limited executive resources you use for self-control **[CORRECT]**
- C. Because the brain can only stay awake for short bursts before needing sleep
- D. Because boredom physically damages neurons

*Explanation:* Focus is effortful because selection requires suppression, and holding attention on non-gripping material taxes the same limited executive resources as self-control and decision-making — plus every switch adds a reconfiguration cost. That's why the answer is rarely "more willpower" and always "a smarter way to spend a finite resource."

### Q5. (Multiple choice) What is the single reframe the whole course is built on?
- A. Focus is a fixed trait you either have or lack
- B. Focus can be forced with enough willpower if you just try harder
- C. Focus is a trainable *capacity* — so the thing to change is your method and environment, not your character **[CORRECT]**
- D. Focus is impossible to improve once you're an adult

*Explanation:* The course's central shift is from character to method: treating focus as a capacity you can train, protect, and spend deliberately moves the problem from "I'm not a focused person" (feels permanent) to "I haven't trained this skill or protected the conditions for it" (changeable). Option D also contradicts the neuroplasticity premise — attention is plastic at any age.

## Module 2 Knowledge Check — The Modern Attention Environment
*Covers Lessons 2.1-2.3. Passing grade 80% (4/5).*

### Q1. (Multiple choice) In the "attention economy," when an app or feed is free to use, what is actually being sold?
- A. Your attention — the time you spend looking is the inventory sold to advertisers **[CORRECT]**
- B. Your personal photos, directly, to the highest bidder
- C. Nothing — free apps are simply a public service
- D. The app's own software code

*Explanation:* "If the product is free, your attention is usually what's being sold." Engagement means time spent looking, and that time is the inventory monetized through advertising. Seeing this clearly is the first step to opting out deliberately.

### Q2. (Multiple choice) Which three design levers do most of the work of keeping you scrolling?
- A. Bright colors, loud sounds, and pop-up ads
- B. Variable rewards (unpredictable payoffs), infinite feeds (no natural stopping point), and notifications (engineered interruptions) **[CORRECT]**
- C. Paywalls, subscriptions, and free trials
- D. Slow loading, forced logins, and CAPTCHAs

*Explanation:* Variable rewards work like a slot machine, infinite feeds remove the natural stopping cue that tells you to quit, and notifications hijack your *orienting* reflex from Module 1. These are deliberate patterns, not accidents — which is why the fix is environmental, not a matter of trying harder.

### Q3. (Multiple choice) Which statement about "multitasking" and switching is best supported by the evidence?
- A. The brain runs two demanding tasks in true parallel with no cost
- B. Switching tasks costs exactly 23 minutes to refocus every time, and multitasking drops your IQ measurably
- C. For demanding tasks, "multitasking" is really fast task-switching, and switch costs plus attention residue are real — but the "40% loss," "IQ drop," and "23 minutes" figures are overstated **[CORRECT]**
- D. Multitasking has no downside as long as you're used to it

*Explanation:* This is the module's science-honesty point. Switch costs and attention residue (part of your mind staying stuck on the last task) are real and replicated. But the dramatic popular figures — the "40% productivity loss," the "IQ drop," the flat "23 minutes to refocus" — are shaky and shouldn't anchor your case. The robust claim is enough: switching is genuinely costly, so protect single-tasking.

### Q4. (True/False) A "quick" three-second question to an AI assistant during focused work is essentially free, because it took so little time.
- A. True
- B. False **[CORRECT]**

*Explanation:* The three-second answer doesn't refund the switch. A reflexive AI check costs *twice*: it's a task-switch (with the full attention-residue tax) and a nudge toward offloading the thinking that builds skill. The reconfiguration cost is roughly the same regardless of how short the detour felt.

### Q5. (Multiple choice) According to Module 2, what makes always-on AI a uniquely powerful distraction?
- A. It is more entertaining than social media
- B. It removes the *friction* of starting a distraction — the small effort that used to give you a moment to catch yourself **[CORRECT]**
- C. It is always wrong, so you waste time correcting it
- D. It physically prevents you from closing the app

*Explanation:* Friction — the small effort of starting a distraction — used to create a beat where you could catch yourself. Always-on AI removes that friction, making it nearly effortless to leave a task. The line that matters is *deliberate vs. reflexive* use, not AI vs. no AI: on purpose it multiplies focus; reached for automatically it's the fastest exit from any task.

## Module 3 Knowledge Check — Training Deep Focus
*Covers Lessons 3.1-3.3. Passing grade 80% (4/5).*

### Q1. (Multiple choice) Why is single-tasking treated as the foundation of the whole training method?
- A. Because doing one thing at a time is morally superior to multitasking
- B. Because a single-task block is the container that makes deep attention possible at all — you can't train a skill you never actually perform **[CORRECT]**
- C. Because it's the fastest way to finish any to-do list
- D. Because multitasking is impossible for everyone

*Explanation:* Single-tasking isn't a virtue badge — it's the necessary container for the reps. A single-task block means one task, one window of time, everything else made genuinely unavailable. Without that container there's no attention to train.

### Q2. (Multiple choice) Instead of relying on willpower to resist checking your phone, what does Module 3 recommend?
- A. Punish yourself each time you slip so you learn faster
- B. Rely on motivation and only work when you feel focused
- C. Remove the option — move the friction so distraction is genuinely expensive to reach, rather than spending discipline fighting the urge **[CORRECT]**
- D. Multitask so you're never tempted to check anything

*Explanation:* Willpower is a decision the attention economy is built to win. Rather than fighting the urge in the moment, design the choice out — put the phone out of reach, close the tab — so distraction costs real effort. You move friction instead of spending discipline.

### Q3. (Multiple choice) When you're doing progressive attention training, what is the actual "rep" you are training?
- A. Staying perfectly focused with your mind never wandering
- B. Noticing that you drifted and returning your attention — over and over **[CORRECT]**
- C. Working for as many hours as possible in one sitting
- D. Feeling no boredom or discomfort at any point

*Explanation:* The rep isn't "never drift" — that's impossible. The rep is the *loop*: catching that your attention wandered and bringing it back. Wandering is the setup for the rep, not a failure. And you return *gently* — self-criticism just spends attention on frustration and makes the practice punishing, which is how people quit.

### Q4. (True/False) A rare two-hour "heroic" focus marathon trains attention better than a short block done daily.
- A. True
- B. False **[CORRECT]**

*Explanation:* Consistency beats intensity. A short daily block accumulates far more reps and becomes an automatic habit; a rare marathon trains almost nothing and mostly teaches you that focus is miserable. Start at a block you can actually *finish* (even 15-20 minutes) — a completed block is a rep, an abandoned ambition is not.

### Q5. (Multiple choice) What is the most accurate description of flow and how you get it?
- A. Flow is a technique you can switch on directly whenever you want
- B. Flow is a myth with no real evidence behind it
- C. Flow is a real state of absorption that you don't chase directly — you build its conditions (clear goal, matched challenge, feedback, no interruptions) and it shows up as a result of trained, protected attention **[CORRECT]**
- D. Flow happens randomly and has nothing to do with attention

*Explanation:* Flow is real and well-reported (though some popular neuroscience claims about it are overstated). You can't force it; you engineer its conditions — with challenge-matched-to-skill as the key lever and *no interruptions* as the non-negotiable, since flow needs uninterrupted time to enter and collapses on a single interruption. That's the deepest reason the protected single-task block matters.

## Module 4 Knowledge Check — Designing a Focus-Protective Life
*Covers Lessons 4.1-4.3. Passing grade 80% (4/5).*

### Q1. (Multiple choice) What is the core principle of "design the environment, not the willpower"?
- A. What's easy and in front of you is what you'll do — so put friction on distraction and remove it from the work **[CORRECT]**
- B. Willpower is unlimited if you believe in yourself
- C. The environment doesn't matter as long as you're motivated
- D. You should redecorate your workspace for aesthetic reasons

*Explanation:* Environment beats intention. Because you'll default to whatever is easy and in reach, the move is to make distraction expensive (phone out of reach, notifications off, unused tabs closed) and the work easy to start. Effort spent *once* on setup saves you from deciding a hundred times a day.

### Q2. (Multiple choice) Why do "rules beat decisions" for protecting attention?
- A. Because rules make you look disciplined to other people
- B. Because a rule made once in advance ("no phone during the morning block") removes the in-the-moment choice the attention economy is built to win **[CORRECT]**
- C. Because you should never make any decisions at all
- D. Because rules are legally binding

*Explanation:* Every in-the-moment "should I check this?" is a decision you can lose. Pre-committing to a rule removes that decision entirely, so you're not spending attention deciding whether to focus. Pair it with time design: protect peak hours, batch shallow work, defend *one* deep block a day rather than attempting five.

### Q3. (Multiple choice) What is the recommended way to use AI during a deep-focus block?
- A. Keep AI open at all times so answers are instant
- B. Keep a "to-ask" list and batch AI into a scheduled slot — like email — and close the AI tab during the block so reflexive reaching costs real effort **[CORRECT]**
- C. Never use AI for anything, ever
- D. Let AI do the load-bearing thinking so you can rest

*Explanation:* Don't give AI on-demand access mid-task — most mid-task questions are reflexes. Batch them into a slot, give AI a *defined job* (summarizing, drafting, reformatting) while keeping the thinking that's actually yours, and guard against automation bias by verifying anything that matters. Designed well, AI is an attention ally; reached for automatically, it's an attention tax (the deliberate-vs-reflexive line again).

### Q4. (True/False) Scrolling social media during a work break is an effective way to recharge your attention.
- A. True
- B. False **[CORRECT]**

*Explanation:* Real breaks are *lower*-input, not higher — a walk, looking out a window, doing nothing. Scrolling just swaps one attention demand for another and doesn't restore you. Attention is rechargeable but not infinite, and how you recover it decides whether the practice lasts.

### Q5. (Multiple choice) Module 4 argues that sleep and stress "set the ceiling" on attention. What does that mean in practice?
- A. If you sleep well you never need to train focus at all
- B. Stress improves focus, so you should stay stressed
- C. No focus technique overcomes a chronically exhausted or stressed brain — so fixing sleep and stress is the highest-leverage move, and it links back to Brain Health 101 **[CORRECT]**
- D. Sleep and stress have no measurable effect on concentration

*Explanation:* Techniques operate *under* a ceiling set by your physiology. A chronically under-slept or over-stressed brain can't focus well no matter how good the method, so fixing the floor is the highest-leverage move — and it's exactly where Deep Focus hands off to Brain Health 101. Sustainability is the whole point: build a rhythm (one reliable daily block, a few firm rules, deliberate recovery, no self-punishment for imperfect days) you'll still keep in three months.

## Module 4 Capstone — Self-Evaluation Rubric (not a Tutor quiz)
*Add this as the final lesson/assignment description. The Week 4 capstone asks the learner to combine their best practice from each module into a **one-page personal focus system**. There's no single right answer, so learners self-score against the rubric below. A strong focus system hits all six.*

Score each item Yes / Partly / Not yet. Aim for six "Yes."

1. **Baseline & trigger (Module 1):** Does your page state your honest starting point — your typical longest clean focus block — and name your single biggest distraction trigger?
2. **Environment rules (Modules 2 & 4):** Have you written specific, pre-committed environment rules (e.g. phone out of reach, notifications off, AI tab closed during the block) rather than vague intentions like "focus more"?
3. **A defined daily deep block (Module 3):** Is there one protected, single-task deep-focus block scheduled at a realistic length you can actually finish — not an ambitious length you'll abandon?
4. **A return habit, not a no-drift fantasy (Module 3):** Does your plan explicitly expect wandering and describe how you'll notice and return *gently*, instead of assuming you'll never lose focus?
5. **A deliberate AI rule (Modules 2 & 4):** Have you decided in advance how AI gets used during focused work (batched, with a defined job) versus kept out — so its use is deliberate, not reflexive?
6. **Recovery & sustainability (Module 4):** Does your system include real, lower-input recovery and account for sleep/stress — and is it modest enough that you'll still be doing it in three months?

*If you scored "Not yet" on any item, revisit that module's final lesson — the fix is there. Your one-page system is what you leave the course with; keep it where you'll see it.*
